# Academic Discussion strategy

## Resolved guidance on student posts

The sources disagree:

- One experienced TOEFL coach recommends ignoring the two student posts to protect time and reports students earning `5/5` with that approach.
- Study.com's TOEFL-prep videos recommend reading both posts, finding an angle not fully covered, and adding value rather than echoing them.
- ETS says the task measures developing ideas, responding to others' viewpoints, and writing in an academic tone. The scoring rubric, however, does not require naming, quoting, agreeing with, or disagreeing with a student.

Keep two standards separate:

- **ETS scoring standard:** the rubric rewards a relevant, developed contribution that responds to the discussion. It does not make naming both students a separate point-bearing requirement.
- **Coaching target:** when both student posts contain usable, distinct ideas, build a discussion-complete response that answers the professor, engages both viewpoints, and adds an original bridge or extension.

Use this coaching default:

1. Read the professor's exact question and answer it immediately with a clear position.
2. Reduce each student post to its core claim in a few words.
3. Connect to Student 1 through agreement, disagreement, qualification, or extension.
4. Add a new mechanism or consequence that goes beyond Student 1.
5. Make that contribution concrete with an example and relevant consequence.
6. Connect the developed idea to Student 2 by resolving, qualifying, or addressing that student's concern.
7. Add a brief limitation or condition when it sharpens the position.
8. Return to the recommended answer or strategy in a concise conclusion when space permits.

The mental map is:

`Professor → Student 1 → new idea/mechanism → example/consequence → Student 2 → nuance → conclusion`

Every student reference must perform a function. A name followed by a summary is not meaningful engagement. The response should extend, connect, qualify, resolve, or challenge the viewpoint.

Use the discussion-complete path only when it remains focused and controlled. If the two posts overlap, one is not useful, or integrating both would produce shallow development, combine their ideas briefly or develop the stronger connection. If time is tight or the posts cause idea paralysis, prioritize a direct answer with one original, well-developed contribution. Never deduct points solely because a student is not named; judge the ETS criteria and report the missed discussion opportunity separately as coaching advice.

## Choose one contribution move

After reading the professor's question, choose one move that adds value instead of merely repeating a student. A brief planning question is: `What useful point has not been developed yet?`

- **Combine:** both students identify real benefits; propose a workable approach that preserves both.
- **Extend:** accept one useful point, then explain a deeper implication or consequence.
- **Missing factor:** identify a relevant factor neither student has developed.
- **Condition:** accept an idea only under a clear condition that prevents a likely problem.
- **Cause–effect:** show how one action produces an immediate result and then a longer chain of consequences.
- **Short–long term:** separate the immediate benefit or cost from the longer-term outcome.
- **Problem–solution:** identify the main obstacle, propose a realistic response, and explain the expected result.

Select the move that produces the clearest developed answer fastest. One move is normally enough; when possible, use that single move to bridge both student viewpoints instead of starting several unrelated arguments. Engaging both students is the coaching preference when it remains natural and developed, while a counterpoint, refinement, or conclusion should appear only when it strengthens the response.

## High-value response shape

Prefer the discussion-complete shape when the prompt and time support it:

1. **Professor:** answer the exact question immediately.
2. **Student 1:** connect to one viewpoint and identify what can be extended or refined.
3. **New contribution:** add a mechanism, condition, consequence, or missing factor.
4. **Support:** make the contribution concrete through an example and consequence.
5. **Student 2:** show how the developed idea addresses, qualifies, or connects to the other viewpoint.
6. **Nuance:** acknowledge one relevant limitation or governing condition.
7. **Conclusion:** return to the main recommendation in one concise sentence.
8. **Proofread:** check spelling, agreement, verb forms, articles, and repeated words.

Use this compact fallback when the full map would reduce clarity or development:

1. **Position:** answer the exact question immediately.
2. **Contribution move:** choose a useful angle such as extend, condition, missing factor, or cause–effect.
3. **Reason:** give one specific reason with a clear causal mechanism.
4. **Development:** make the reason concrete through an example, consequence, comparison, or brief experience.
5. **Nuance:** optionally acknowledge a limitation or another viewpoint in one sentence.
6. **Close:** optionally finish with a short synthesis when time allows.
7. **Proofread:** reserve the final moments for spelling, agreement, verb forms, articles, and repeated words.

Development is more valuable than mentioning every participant mechanically. A concrete example shows who, what, or how; `This is beneficial` and `My friend improved` are conclusions, not developed examples.

## Partial agreement

A middle or qualified position is allowed; the ETS rubric does not require choosing one student's side. Use it only when the final judgment is easy to identify and can be developed within the time limit. A response that says both sides have merit and then gives a specific compromise or condition can be strong. A response that keeps switching sides without resolving the question is unfocused.

When partial agreement creates extra branches, simplify to one main recommendation and one governing condition. Do not deduct points merely for the phrase `I partially agree`.

## Language strategy

- Prefer controlled, natural vocabulary over ambitious words used inaccurately.
- Create variety through sentence function and shape: claim, explanation, example, consequence, concession.
- Mix concise sentences with one or two well-controlled complex structures.
- Use connectors because they express a real relationship, not to decorate the response.
- Remove repeated thesis sentences and generic opening filler; spend those words developing the reason.
- Treat natural collocations and controlled clause variety as evidence of range; difficult vocabulary and long sentences are not goals by themselves.

## Time and length

Prep videos describe a 10-minute response and recommend at least 100 words. Treat displayed test instructions as authoritative. For practice, aim for enough text to develop one idea fully—often roughly 110–150 words—without sacrificing accuracy or proofreading. Word count is not an independent criterion in the published ETS scoring rubric.

## Claims excluded from the strategy

- Do not call the Writing section adaptive. ETS specifications say Writing is linear; Reading and Listening are the adaptive sections.
- Do not describe the Academic Discussion task itself as a `1–6` score. Its raw response score is `0–5`; `1–6` is the reported section scale.
- Do not make a counterpoint, conclusion, personal example, or named student reference mandatory. They are optional tools for satisfying the actual rubric.
