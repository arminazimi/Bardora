# Write an Email strategy and language bank

Use this reference for planning, drafting, revising, and coaching the 2026 Write an Email task. Treat the phrases as adaptable functions, not sentences to memorize unchanged.

## Function-first workflow

Before writing, extract:

1. **Relationship:** friend, classmate, professor, employer, business, landlord, administrator, or community contact.
2. **Purpose:** complain, request, inquire, apologize, advise, organize, suggest, thank, follow up, recommend, or ask permission.
3. **Required content:** convert every prompt bullet into a checkable item.
4. **Relevant facts:** what happened, when, where, what was expected, and what has already been tried.
5. **Impact:** why the situation matters to the sender or recipient.
6. **Action:** the specific, realistic response requested from the recipient.
7. **Register:** friendly, neutral-professional, or formal.

Build the email as:

`purpose → facts/context → impact → requested action or next step → polite close`

Each sentence should perform at least one of these functions. Generic gratitude or formality should not displace a required prompt bullet.

## Register choices

### Friendly

Use contractions and direct warmth: `I know how difficult this can feel`, `You could try`, `If you'd like, we can...`.

### Neutral-professional

Use direct, courteous language: `I am writing to ask about...`, `Could you please confirm...`, `I would appreciate your assistance with...`.

### Formal

Use precise requests and restrained tone: `I am writing to request...`, `Would it be possible to...`, `Thank you for considering my request.`

Match tone to the relationship. Formality cannot compensate for a missing purpose, weak detail, or unclear request.

### Greetings and sign-offs

| Relationship | Greeting | Sign-off |
|---|---|---|
| Friend or close peer | `Hi [First Name],` | `Best,`, `Take care,`, `Thanks,` |
| Classmate or familiar contact | `Hi [First Name],` or `Hello [First Name],` | `Best,`, `All the best,` |
| Professor, employer, or authority figure | `Dear [Title] [Last Name],` | `Sincerely,`, `Best regards,`, `Kind regards,` |
| Role-based recipient | `Dear [Role/Title],` or `Hello,` | `Sincerely,`, `Kind regards,` |

Use the name and title supplied by the prompt. A greeting and sign-off should fit the relationship without consuming space needed for required content.

## Task-family menus

Use the scenario bank for topic vocabulary and these five families for the email's communicative shape.

### 1. Request help or action

`situation → specific need → realistic request`

- Situation: `I am writing because I need assistance with [specific issue].`
- Need: `The main challenge is [problem and impact].`
- Request: `Would you be available to [specific action]?`
- Request: `I was wondering if you could [base verb].`
- Deadline: `I would appreciate it if you could let me know by [relevant time].`

### 2. Give feedback or suggest an improvement

`experience → evidence → improvement → expected benefit`

- Experience: `I recently [attended/used/visited] [thing], and I wanted to share some feedback.`
- Strength: `One aspect that worked particularly well was [detail].`
- Issue: `However, I noticed that [specific observable problem].`
- Suggestion: `One improvement that might help is [action].`
- Benefit: `This change could [reduce confusion/improve access/save time].`

Describe the experience and impact rather than labeling it `terrible`, `useless`, or similar.

### 3. Offer advice or perspective

`empathy → recommendation → reason/tradeoff → next step or support`

- Empathy: `I can understand why this feels like a difficult decision.`
- Recommendation: `Based on what you described, I would suggest [option] because [reason].`
- Tradeoff: `One advantage is [benefit]; however, [limitation].`
- Alternative: `Another option you might consider is [alternative].`
- Support: `If you'd like, we could [helpful action] together.`

### 4. Recruit, invite, or recommend

`initiative → need/fit → value → call to action`

- Initiative: `I am writing to let you know about [event/project].`
- Fit: `Your experience with [skill] would make you a strong fit.`
- Need: `We are looking for someone who can [specific contribution].`
- Invitation: `Would you be interested in joining us?`
- Next step: `Could we arrange a time to discuss how you might contribute?`

### 5. Navigate disagreement or constraints

`acknowledgment → constraint → alternative/compromise → next step`

- Acknowledge: `I understand why this is important to you.`
- Constraint: `Unfortunately, [specific reason] makes it difficult to [action].`
- Alternative: `What I can do instead is [realistic option].`
- Compromise: `Would it help if we [alternative]?`
- Next step: `Could we discuss this and agree on a workable solution?`

State the constraint honestly without sounding dismissive. An alternative is useful only when it is feasible and responds to the other person's underlying need.

| Advice about a new university or friends | empathy → practical options → brief experience → offer support | `settle in`, `adjust to campus life`, `make connections`, `join clubs`, `attend events`, `get involved on campus` | advice modals; conditional; past experience |

| Internship, program, or workshop inquiry | identify opportunity → ask duties/timing/eligibility → documents/deadline → next steps | `application process`, `eligibility`, `required documents`, `apply for a position`, `meet the requirements`, `gain experience` | indirect questions; present simple for published facts; `could/would` |
| Academic interview or research help | introduce project → explain relevance → request interview/help → scheduling flexibility | `conduct an interview`, `primary sources`, `field of study`, `gather data`, `consult an expert`, `academic guidance` | infinitive of purpose; polite conditionals; formal register |

| Donation for a community event | introduce group/event → purpose/beneficiaries → specific donation request → logistics/follow-up | `charity event`, `raise money for`, `families in need`, `make a donation`, `contribute to the cause`, `basic supplies` | future reference; purpose clause; `Would you be willing to...` |

## Adaptable sentence frames

### Purpose

- `I am writing to ask about [specific issue].`
- `I am writing to request [specific information/action].`
- `I am writing to report [specific problem].`
- `I wanted to thank you for [specific help].`
- `I am writing to follow up on [meeting/application/conversation].`

### Facts and impact

- `According to [record/notice/agreement], [expected fact].`
- `I [action] on [date], but [problem].`
- `This has affected [project, schedule, safety, payment, or access] because [concrete consequence].`
- `I have already tried [action], but the issue has continued.`

### Requests and solutions

- `Could you please [base verb] by [relevant time]?`
- `Would it be possible to [base verb]?`
- `I would appreciate it if you could [base verb].`
- `Would you be willing to [base verb]?`
- `If approved, I will [safety/logistics commitment].`
- `Please let me know whether [indirect question].`

### Closings

- `Thank you for your time and assistance.`
- `Thank you for considering my request.`
- `I would appreciate an update when convenient.`
- `Please let me know if you need any additional information.`
- `I look forward to your response.`
- Friendly: `If you'd like, we can talk this weekend.`

Choose a closing that fits the purpose. In a complaint or urgent request, pair courtesy with a concrete requested action rather than relying on `as soon as possible` alone.

## Grammar controls

- `Could you please + base verb`: `Could you please confirm the amount?`
- `Would you mind + gerund`: `Would you mind checking the payroll records?`
- `I would appreciate it if you could + base verb`.
- `apologize for + noun/gerund`: `I apologize for missing the meeting.`
- `because + clause`; `because of/due to + noun phrase`.
- `look forward to + noun/gerund`: `I look forward to hearing from you.`
- Use past simple for completed events, present perfect for a past action with a current or continuing effect, and future forms for plans or commitments.
- Check agreement, articles, countable plurals, prepositions, names, dates, amounts, and location phrases during proofreading.

## Coaching and revision rules

- Build from the prompt bullets, not from the closest memorized scenario.
- Keep only details that support the purpose, impact, or requested action.
- Invent plausible neutral details only when the task permits and they do not contradict the prompt.
- Replace fixed personal names with the supplied name or a neutral placeholder.
- Treat openers and closers as frames. Vary them when repetition, register, or task purpose calls for a different sentence.
- Prefer one clear request over several vague demands.
- Express urgency with a date, risk, or consequence when the prompt supports it.
- `Thank you in advance` is usable but can sound presumptive; prefer `Thank you for considering my request` when compliance is uncertain.
- A subject line is optional unless the prompt or interface requires one. Do not spend response space on an invented subject when the task expects only the email body.
- Use questions or polite conditionals for requests rather than commands.
- Select two or three reliable frames per task family and vary the specific content; do not reuse one full template for every prompt.
- Complete every frame with a concrete detail from the prompt or a plausible detail the task allows.

## Email self-check

Before submitting, answer:

1. Did I cover every prompt bullet?
2. Can the recipient identify my purpose from the opening?
3. Did I give enough relevant detail to understand the situation and impact?
4. Is my requested action specific, realistic, and appropriately polite?
5. Does the tone fit the relationship, and are the key verb patterns correct?
