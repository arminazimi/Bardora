# High-score sample calibration

## Evidence boundary

Use the real section result as supporting calibration for the learner's overall level and habits. Do not treat any practice response as the real exam answer or its Claude score as an ETS score. The exact Build a Sentence performance and real exam responses are unavailable.

A second user-provided corpus contains a much larger set of Academic Discussion practice responses attributed to another learner reported to have earned `6/6` in the real Writing section. Apply the same evidence boundary: the reported section result shows that noticeable local problems can coexist with strong overall performance, but it does not certify every sentence, factual claim, or collocation in the practice corpus.

## Transferable patterns

Across the samples, the useful pattern is controlled development rather than a magic phrase:

1. State a position that answers the professor's question.
2. Explain the mechanism: why the claim is true or how the result occurs.
3. Add a concrete, imaginable example rather than a generic assertion.
4. Follow the example to a relevant consequence when space allows.
5. Use transitions to express real logical relationships.
6. Proofread local spelling, agreement, verb-form, and article errors.

For top-band calibration, look for relevant and well-elaborated ideas, syntactic variety under control, natural word combinations, and only minor errors that do not obscure meaning. One isolated typo does not mechanically lower a response by half a band; apply the rubric holistically.

## Lessons from the larger practice corpus

Transfer these habits when they improve the response:

- answer the issue immediately and keep the position identifiable;
- connect to a classmate's point, then add a mechanism or consequence;
- create syntactic range through function: explanation, comparison, concession, condition, and result;
- use a causal chain such as `choice → immediate effect → practical consequence`;
- reuse a small number of controlled structures across topics while changing the actual reasoning;
- close only when the final sentence synthesizes the argument rather than repeating it.

Do not copy the corpus mechanically. Repeated frames such as `From my standpoint`, `favoring the idea that`, `Needless to say`, and `Contrary to [Name]'s statement` can make several responses sound formulaic. Rotate only when the rhetorical relationship is real, and prefer a shorter direct sentence when it is clearer.

### Language repair bank

When these source expressions appear in a learner's writing, teach the natural alternative rather than endorsing the original as “score-boosting vocabulary”:

| Avoid or repair | Prefer |
|---|---|
| `expand one's eyesight` | `broaden one's perspective` |
| `at the comfort of one's independence` | `independently` / `at one's own pace` |
| `dissipate time or money` | `waste time or money` / `consume substantial resources` |
| `be subject to wrong information` | `be exposed to inaccurate information` |
| `purify one's mind from negative thoughts` | `reduce financial stress` / `ease one's worries` |
| `livelihood supplies` | `basic necessities` / `living expenses` |
| `dim eyesight` or `deleterious waves` | `eye strain from prolonged screen use` |
| `irreparable damages` | `irreparable damage` only when the harm is genuinely irreversible |
| `expedite the solution of a problem` | `help resolve a problem more quickly` |
| `keep to their schedule` for planning ability | `manage their time` / `stay on schedule` |
| `a myriad of information` | `a great deal of information` / `many sources of information` |

### Claim-quality filter

High-band language cannot rescue an implausible or prejudicial claim. Flag and repair:

- unsupported medical or neuroscience claims;
- invented causal certainty, such as guaranteeing health, salary, happiness, or career success;
- speculative examples presented as likely facts, such as space exploration exposing humanity to aliens;
- categorical claims that AI systems cannot cause harm merely because humans created them;
- stereotypes that associate nationality, refugee status, age, religion, or another group identity with crime, incompetence, or social threat;
- false dilemmas that ignore a reasonable middle condition.

Replace such claims with a narrow, defensible mechanism. The coaching goal is a plausible example and controlled inference, not a dramatic assertion.

## Nuanced positions and partial agreement

Several samples use a middle or partial-agreement position. ETS does not prohibit this. A nuanced position can score highly when the writer's final judgment is precise and the response remains focused.

Use a qualified position only when the writer can finish this sentence clearly: `My recommendation is [specific choice or condition] because [main reason].` If the response merely alternates between both sides, develops too many branches, or never resolves the issue, revise it to a clearer position. Treat partial agreement as a focus and time-management choice, not a scoring violation.

## Natural language bank

Learn word combinations in context rather than isolated difficult words. Useful patterns observed or highlighted in the article include:

- `lead to [result]`
- `depend on [factor]`
- `in a balanced manner`
- `make [a place/system] more accommodating`
- `give [a building/program] a new purpose`
- `preserve / hold on to cultural heritage`
- `restore my energy`
- `regain a sense of control`
- `spiral out of control`
- `lean toward [an option]`
- `in its entirety`
- `navigate the world`
- `the solution lies in [approach]`

Use only combinations that fit the prompt and the writer can control accurately. Prefer a familiar natural collocation to an ambitious but uncertain phrase.

Useful structural options include conditionals, relative clauses, comparisons, multi-clause sentences, participial phrases, `not only ... but also`, `even though`, and `rather than`. Variety helps only when meaning remains clear; one or two controlled complex structures are enough.

The comparative-correlative pattern can be useful when both clauses express a credible relationship:

`The more consistently students practice a skill, the more confident they are likely to become.`

Avoid using `the more ..., the more ...` to make an unsupported universal claim. Add `likely to` or `tend to` when the relationship is probabilistic.

## Email calibration

The Email sample's transferable sequence is:

`purpose → relevant facts and urgency → concrete impact → clear request → polite close`

The model opens directly, explains the missed delivery and project deadline, shows why the delay matters, and asks for a practical response. When grading, prioritize task completion, register, clarity, organization, and language control over copying a fixed greeting or closing.
