# Academic Discussion language menus

Use these menus to select a natural frame for a specific rhetorical function. A frame is a launchpad: complete it with prompt-specific content, mechanism, or evidence.

## Selection rules

- Choose at most one frame for each function the response genuinely needs.
- Prefer direct frames over ornate alternatives under time pressure.
- Paraphrase the student's idea in original words; quoting or copying does not add a contribution.
- Make the sentence after a transition prove the logical relationship it announces.
- Rotate a small set of controlled frames across practice, while varying the actual content.
- Remove a frame when the same meaning can be expressed more clearly without it.

## State a position

### Direct

- `I believe [position] because [specific reason].`
- `In my view, [position].`
- `From my perspective, [position].`

### Qualified

- `While both sides have merit, I believe [clear judgment].`
- `Although [limitation], I would still argue that [position].`
- `This approach is effective only when [condition].`

### Connect immediately to a student

- `I agree with [Name] that [brief paraphrase], and I would add that [new angle].`
- `I think [Name] is right about [brief point], but the larger issue is [extension].`
- `Like [Name], I believe [paraphrase]; however, [qualification or new factor].`

## Agree and extend

- `To build on [Name]'s point, [new mechanism or consequence].`
- `In addition to what [Name] mentioned, [new factor].`
- `[Name] highlights [brief idea], which is especially important because [mechanism].`
- `[Name]'s point becomes even stronger when we consider [condition or consequence].`

Agreement must lead somewhere new. A sentence that only says `I agree` is acknowledgment, not development.

## Disagree or qualify

### Acknowledge before countering

- `While [Name] raises a fair point about [idea], [counterclaim].`
- `I understand [Name]'s concern that [paraphrase], but [response].`
- `Although [Name]'s view is reasonable in some cases, [clear limitation].`
- `I respectfully disagree with [Name]'s claim that [paraphrase] because [reason].`

### Explain the gap

- `What this argument overlooks is [missing factor].`
- `The main limitation of this view is [specific problem].`
- `This may be true when [condition], but [different condition or outcome].`
- `In practice, [contrasting mechanism or evidence].`

## Support a claim

### Explain the mechanism

- `This matters because [consequence].`
- `The reason is that [mechanism].`
- `One important factor is [factor], which [effect].`
- `In other words, [clearer causal explanation].`

### Introduce an example

- `For example, [specific situation].`
- `A clear illustration is [case with relevant detail].`
- `Consider a situation in which [concrete scenario].`
- `For instance, [actor] could [action], which would [consequence].`

### Follow the example

- `As a result, [outcome that supports the position].`
- `This would make it more likely that [relevant consequence].`
- `The example shows that [precise inference].`
- `Over time, [longer-term effect].`

## Connect the second viewpoint

- `This development also addresses [Name]'s concern about [issue] because [bridge].`
- `At the same time, [Name] is right that [limitation], so [condition or solution].`
- `This approach combines [Name 1]'s emphasis on [idea] with [Name 2]'s concern about [idea].`
- `Unlike [Name]'s proposal, this option would [specific comparative advantage].`

Use the second viewpoint to refine, test, or strengthen the argument. Name-dropping without a bridge is not substantive engagement.

## Flow and conclusion

### Contrast or qualification

- `However, [real contrast].`
- `That said, [qualification].`
- `At the same time, [simultaneous consideration].`
- `On the other hand, [genuine alternative].`

### Return to the judgment

- `Ultimately, [refined position].`
- `For these reasons, [concise answer to the professor].`
- `Therefore, [conclusion that follows from the mechanism].`

A conclusion is optional. Use it only when it synthesizes or refines the position; omit a sentence that merely repeats the opening.

## Controlled grammar for range

Use at most one or two of these patterns in a short response. Select them because they express the intended logic, not because they are assumed to earn points automatically.

### Comparative correlation

- `The more [subject] [verb], the more [result].`
- `The less [subject] [verb], the more likely [result] becomes.`

Check parallel structure and avoid absolute causation. Example: `The more regularly students receive specific feedback, the more likely they are to recognize recurring errors.`

### Not only ... but also

- `Not only does [singular subject] [base verb], but it also [verb].`
- `Not only do [plural subject] [base verb], but they also [verb].`
- `[Subject] not only [verb] but also [parallel verb].`

After initial `Not only`, use auxiliary inversion. Keep the two elements grammatically parallel.

### Result participles

- `[Main clause], allowing [actor] to [verb].`
- `[Main clause], enabling [actor] to [verb].`
- `[Main clause], reducing/increasing [result].`
- `[Main clause], making it easier for [actor] to [verb].`

The understood actor and causal relationship must be clear. Do not attach an `-ing` clause to an unrelated subject or use it to claim a result the main clause does not support.

### Concession

- `Although [real limitation], [main judgment].`
- `While [competing benefit] may be valuable, [reason for final position].`
- `Even if [condition], [remaining limitation or conclusion].`

The two clauses must genuinely contrast. A decorative concession does not demonstrate range.

## Coaching use

When the learner asks for language help, provide:

1. two or three frames matched to the missing functions in the draft;
2. one completed prompt-specific example for each selected frame;
3. a short substitution drill requiring the learner to reuse the function with new content.

Do not dump the full menu into ordinary feedback. Prioritize the frames the learner can control and immediately apply.
